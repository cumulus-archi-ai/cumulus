Software health The triple-debt model — how Cumulus helps

In the AI era, software health is more than clean code. Storey's triple-debt model names three interacting debts — technical (in code), cognitive (in people's shared understanding) and intent (in recorded rationale). A system stays healthy only while the three stay aligned. As a planning & design tool, Cumulus works mainly on the intent and cognitive edges while keeping technical trade-offs deliberate.

Technical debt

in code & architecture

What it is. Implementation shortcuts that make a system harder and riskier to change — and AI-generated code accumulates them faster than ever.

Cumulus contributions
  • Workload-driven sizing. Compute, databases and cache are derived from request rate × CPU-ms ÷ target utilisation — not guessed instance counts — avoiding the mis-provisioning that forces re-architecture.
  • Priced, deliberate trade-offs. Skipping HA, under-sizing or deferring DR each show their cost delta, so every shortcut is a quantified choice rather than an accident.
  • Sanity checks & foresight. The validator flags risky designs, and Conservative / Base / Aggressive scenarios expose scaling cliffs before they force an emergency rewrite.

Cognitive debt

in people

What it is. Erosion of the team's shared understanding of why the system is built as it is — which AI accelerates by shipping features faster than people can absorb them.

Cumulus contributions
  • No black box. The methodology panel exposes every flat rate, multiplier and catalog, and each rate's “?” traces exactly which cards consume it — so the logic is inspectable, not folklore.
  • Live cause and effect. Every input change ripples instantly to the total, per-user economics and breakdown, so the team keeps an accurate mental model of the system.
  • Shared vocabulary. Per-user and per-1k-request economics plus self-documenting inputs let a new teammate reconstruct the reasoning without tribal knowledge.

Intent debt

in externalized artifacts

What it is. Missing or eroded rationale, goals and constraints — the knowledge humans and AI agents need to change a system safely. The least visible debt, and in the AI era often the most damaging.

Cumulus contributions
  • Assumptions register. Every workload, reliability, security, AI and commercial input is captured as a named assumption rather than living only in someone's head.
  • Durable design artifact. One click exports a report recording the goals, constraints, rationale and full cost breakdown — commit it beside the code as the design's source of intent.
  • Captured constraints & provenance. SLA, compliance, DR, purchasing and budget are recorded as the constraints that shaped the architecture — timestamped, attributed, with any rate overrides preserved.

New here? How Cumulus helps you plan & design

The lesson under the story: an architect should settle intent (what we're building and why) and cognition (one shared picture of it) before the technical implementation — and then keep all three in sync as the design changes. Cumulus is the place to do that. Its figures use blended, illustrative public-cloud rates for relative planning and design — not a billing quote — so always validate the headline assumptions (request rate, egress, AI token volumes) against measured data and your provider's pricing before committing.

Costs How these numbers are calculated

These figures are not a quote from AWS, Azure, GCP or any single provider. Cumulus uses one vendor-neutral, blended rate card — representative mid-tier on-demand prices averaged across the major clouds, expressed in USD per month — so estimates stay provider-agnostic and useful for relative comparison and design rather than exact procurement.

How a single number is built:

  1. Convert inputs to resource quantities — vCPUs, GB of RAM, GB stored, GB of egress, requests, provisioned IOPS, serverless invocations & GB-seconds, queue messages and streaming GB, warehouse TB scanned, AI tokens and GPU-hours.
  2. Price each quantity — flat unit rates for infrastructure; per-million-token, per-vector and per-GPU-hour prices for AI.
  3. Apply multipliers — runtime & ARM, database model / storage tier, cache engine, availability target, DR posture, the chosen region price index (and any extra active regions), and non-prod environments.
  4. Sum per category — compute (incl. platform overhead), databases, cache, object (incl. file storage), data & messaging, networking & edge, observability, security, AI and DR.
  5. Apply the commercial layer — commitment/spot discounts, any enterprise discount, support fees, credits, third-party SaaS & licensing, an amortised one-time / migration cost, and tax — to produce the net payable. Totals can be displayed in several currencies (illustrative FX).

Everything that feeds those steps is laid out below: the complete flat-rate card (grouped by category), the multipliers (grouped by what they affect), the rate catalogs (GPUs, LLM tokens, database engines, compliance frameworks, regions), and the AI / ML default prices. You can override any flat unit rate to match your own negotiated pricing — the whole estimate recalculates instantly, and Reset defaults restores the blended values.

    01 Users & Traffic ?

    The full funnel from total accounts down to peak concurrency. Concurrency and request rate are derived from real behaviour (sessions × length × intensity) rather than guessed — so capacity rests on measurable activity.

    Jahidul & his team — read this as a story first

    Meet Jahidul, the tech lead, and the team behind AUBRIA.ai. They're about to size the cloud for AUBRIA.ai — but before a single number goes in, Jahidul gathers everyone at the whiteboard and walks them through the same story this card tells.

    1. "We've had a good year," Jahidul begins — every account that ever signed up to AUBRIA.ai is their Registered users. "But most of them," he reminds the team, "aren't with us today."
    2. The growth lead pulls the real figures: the people who came back this month are their Monthly active (MAU), and on an average day only a slice of those log in — the Daily active (DAU). Jahidul writes the ratio on the board, Stickiness (DAU/MAU): "that's how habitual we really are."
    3. A backend engineer adds how people actually use it: each active user opens the app a handful of times a day — Sessions / user / day — and stays for the Avg session length on each visit.
    4. "And while they're in," Jahidul says, "they hit us at some Requests / user / min — that's the intensity of a single user."
    5. The team multiplies it out — active users × sessions × length × intensity — to see how many people are typically online at once, their Avg concurrent, and the steady backend load, Derived load (RPS).
    6. Then the SRE raises a hand: "Launch day won't be average." Their busiest moment runs hotter by the Peak-to-average factor, which sets Peak concurrent — the spike Jahidul's architecture has to survive (leave it on auto, or type their own).
    7. Finally Jahidul looks ahead: User growth compounds month over month, so the peak they design for today is just next quarter's baseline — which is why the team checks the projection before committing budget.

    So when the AUBRIA.ai team fills the fields below, they aren't guessing — they're writing their own story in numbers. The dashed grey fields (Stickiness, Avg concurrent, Derived load) Cumulus works out for them.

    02 Application Profile ?

    A complete solution is usually several services — a web tier, a REST API, workers, an AI service. Add a component for each; every one is sized and costed separately, then summed. Traffic share is the % of total requests it handles (tiers can overlap — a page load can hit both the web tier and the API).

    Jahidul & his team — read this as a story first

    Back at the whiteboard, Jahidul reminds the team that AUBRIA.ai isn't one program — it's several services working together. This card is where they describe each one, because Cumulus sizes and costs every component on its own profile, then adds them up.

    1. They list the moving parts of AUBRIA.ai — a web tier, the REST API, background workers, the AI inference service. Each becomes a workload component with its own name and type, and Jahidul adds a row per service with + Add workload component.
    2. Not every request touches every service, so for each one they note its Traffic share — the slice of total requests it handles. ("Tiers overlap," Jahidul notes: a single page load hits both the web tier and the API, so shares can sum past 100%.)
    3. A backend engineer estimates how hard each request works the processor — the CPU time / req in milliseconds — the single biggest lever on how many cores that service needs.
    4. They pick how much memory each core carries, the RAM / vCPU, so memory-hungry services — the AI tier especially — get fatter instances.
    5. Each service runs on a stack, so they set its Runtime (Node, Python, Java, Go…), which nudges efficiency, and its CPU arch — where moving to ARM shaves roughly 15% off compute.
    6. The SRE points out that a lot of AUBRIA.ai's traffic is cacheable, so they enter a Cache / CDN hit rate — every cached hit is a request the origin never has to compute.
    7. Some work doesn't need an instant reply, so they mark the Async load — the share of requests handed to background workers and queues instead of the live path.
    8. Finally they note the Response size each service returns, because every byte leaving the cloud turns into egress cost further down the design.
    9. Not everything is a sized server, though: the backend lead adds AUBRIA.ai's platform overhead — the serverless functions behind webhooks and cron (invocations × duration × memory, plus any always-warm provisioned concurrency), the Kubernetes control-plane fee per cluster, the container registry, and the CI/CD build minutes the team burns shipping every day.

    Add a component for every part of AUBRIA.ai and Cumulus sizes each on its own profile, then sums the fleet — so the compute bill reflects the real shape of their system, not one averaged guess.

    03 Storage & I/O ?

    Growth and read/write profile, object storage with lifecycle tiering, plus the I/O and internal-transfer lines that often get missed. Per-database size, storage tier and IOPS now live with each database in the next section.

    Jahidul & his team — read this as a story first

    With the services mapped, Jahidul turns the team to the data AUBRIA.ai creates and moves — the part, he warns, that teams most often under-count.

    1. Every day AUBRIA.ai writes new records — embeddings, logs, user content — so the team estimates the Total DB growth / month across all databases: the GB their datastores put on each month.
    2. They note how the product reads versus writes — the Read / write mix. AUBRIA.ai turns out read-heavy, a hint that caching and read replicas will pay off later.
    3. Beyond the databases, AUBRIA.ai keeps files — uploads, model artifacts, exports — in Object storage, sized in GB.
    4. Much of it is rarely touched, so they set a Cold / archive share to drop that slice onto cheaper archival tiers.
    5. Storage isn't the only charge — every read, write and list is billed — so they estimate Object operations per month.
    6. A couple of services need a real shared file system, not object storage, so they note Shared / file storage (EFS/FSx) in GB — convenient, but the SRE flags that it costs roughly ten times object storage per GB, so they keep it to what genuinely needs it.
    7. Then the line the SRE always circles: Monthly egress, the GB AUBRIA.ai ships out of the cloud to users and partner APIs — usually the most expensive data of all.
    8. And services talking across zones isn't free, so they capture Inter-AZ / internal transfer — the cross-zone traffic that quietly stacks up once AUBRIA.ai runs highly available.

    04 Databases & Caching ?

    Most solutions run several datastores — a primary relational DB, a cache, a search index, maybe a vector or analytics store. Add a database for each, pick its engine from the catalog (75+), set its size, tier, HA and load share; each is sized and costed separately, then summed.

    Jahidul & his team — read this as a story first

    Now the heart of AUBRIA.ai's data layer. Just as the app is several services, Jahidul reminds the team it leans on several datastores — and each gets its own row here, sized and costed on its own.

    1. They add a row for every store AUBRIA.ai relies on — a primary relational DB, a search index, the vector store behind embeddings — each with its own name and engine chosen from the catalog.
    2. For each one they pick a Deployment model — fully managed, self-managed, or serverless — trading operational effort against price.
    3. They set its Size in GB: today's footprint, which the growth from the last card compounds over the months ahead.
    4. The Storage tier decides how fast and how pricey those GB are — standard SSD, high-throughput provisioned-IOPS, or cold HDD — and for the IOPS-bound stores they pin a Peak IOPS.
    5. Not every datastore carries the same traffic, so each gets a Load share — the fraction of database work it handles.
    6. Because AUBRIA.ai came out read-heavy, they add Read replicas to spread reads, and switch on High availability where downtime isn't an option — both multiply that database's compute.
    7. In front of the hot path sits the cache: they choose a Cache engine (Redis, Valkey…) and a Cache size — every GB served from cache is load the databases never feel (set it to 0 to switch caching off).
    8. Finally they cap the Peak DB connections the fleet must sustain at once, so connection pools and instance sizing line up with AUBRIA.ai's real concurrency.

    Add a datastore for every part of AUBRIA.ai's data layer and Cumulus sizes each on its own engine, tier and HA, then sums them — while the cache simply shrinks what those databases have to carry.

    05 Networking & Edge ?

    The plumbing around your traffic — gateways, addresses, private links, DNS and the CDN. These are mostly small hourly and per-GB meters that stay invisible until the invoice arrives. All scale with the chosen region.

    Jahidul & his team — read this as a story first

    With the workload sized, the backend lead and SRE turn to how AUBRIA.ai actually talks — to the world, to managed services, and to itself. Outbound volume was set back in Data & I/O; here they price the plumbing around it, the part that quietly fills a real cloud bill.

    1. Private subnets can't reach the internet on their own, so they add a NAT gateway — billed per hour and per GB processed — and any public-facing host needs a Public IPv4, now charged hourly.
    2. To reach managed databases and queues privately they add VPC endpoints / PrivateLink, and to bridge to the office or a partner cloud, a VPN / interconnect tunnel.
    3. In front of the app sits an API Gateway (per million requests) and managed DNS — a hosted zone plus per-query charges for every lookup of AUBRIA.ai.
    4. To serve users quickly and cut origin load, a CDN delivers cached responses (per GB and per million requests) — the very edge that Application Profile's cache-hit % was already crediting on the compute side.
    5. And the load balancer doesn't just sit there — it processes data, billed per GB that flows through it.

    These are the meters teams forget until the invoice lands. Cumulus surfaces them so AUBRIA.ai's Network line is honest — and because they're real infrastructure, the chosen region's price index scales them too.

    06 Messaging & Analytics ?

    The data plane between your services: queues and streams that move events, the pipelines that process them, and the warehouse that answers analytical queries. Priced separately from your databases and object storage.

    Jahidul & his team — read this as a story first

    Back in the Application Profile the team marked how much of AUBRIA.ai runs off the request path. That async work has to flow somewhere — so here the backend lead and the data engineer price the messaging and analytics plane that carries it.

    1. Events between services ride a queue or topic — order placed, document uploaded, model finished — so they estimate queue / topic messages per month, the same async load Application Profile hinted at, now given a price.
    2. High-volume telemetry and activity streams go through a streaming pipeline, billed per GB ingested; if they run their own Kafka / MSK cluster instead, that's a per-broker hourly fee.
    3. Raw events rarely arrive analysis-ready, so a data engineer runs ETL / processing jobs — billed per processing hour — to clean and reshape them.
    4. The results land in an analytics warehouse: they pay for warehouse storage (per GB) and, separately, for warehouse queries by the terabyte scanned — the line that surprises teams when a dashboard refreshes too often.

    This plane is invisible in a database-only estimate, yet for an AI product fed by events and analytics it's often substantial. Cumulus gives it its own category so AUBRIA.ai's data movement is costed, not assumed — and the region's price index scales it like the rest of the infrastructure.

    07 Reliability & Disaster Recovery ?

    Availability sizes in-zone redundancy; the DR strategy sizes what happens when a whole region fails. RTO (how fast you recover) and RPO (how much data you can lose) drive the strategy and backup cadence — and the cost.

    Jahidul & his team — read this as a story first

    With the system designed, Jahidul asks the harder question: what happens when it breaks? Reliability, he tells the team, isn't a feature you bolt on later for AUBRIA.ai — it's a dial they have to set on purpose.

    1. First the day-to-day: how much downtime AUBRIA.ai can tolerate in normal operation — the Availability target (SLA). Reaching for 99.99% instead of 99.9% means more redundancy across zones, and more compute to pay for it.
    2. Then the bigger fear: what if a whole region goes dark? Their DR strategy decides how much of AUBRIA.ai stands ready elsewhere — from cheap backup-and-restore, through pilot-light and warm standby, up to a hot active-active twin.
    3. Two promises drive that choice. The RPO target is how much data AUBRIA.ai can afford to lose — minutes? an hour? — which sets how often they must capture state.
    4. The RTO target is how fast they must be back online — and a tight RTO is what pushes them from plain backups toward a warm or hot standby.
    5. Those promises set the Backup frequency: daily snapshots are cheap, but a strict RPO may demand 6-hourly, hourly, or continuous capture.
    6. Finally they decide how long to keep those copies — the Backup retention in days — balancing recovery windows and audit needs against storage cost.
    7. None of this is safe if they're flying blind, so the SRE budgets Observability properly: distributed tracing / APM across AUBRIA.ai's services, the custom metrics the team actually watches, synthetic checks that page them before users notice, and a log-retention window long enough to debug an incident and satisfy auditors.

    Jahidul sets AUBRIA.ai's RPO and RTO honestly, and Cumulus prices the redundancy, standby, backups and the observability those promises actually require — so reliability is a costed decision, not a hope.

    08 Security & Compliance ?

    Edge defenses, security tooling, and the compliance regimes you must attest to. Each framework carries amortised audit and control-tooling cost; selecting any one also adds a continuous-compliance monitoring platform. Managed DDoS is a flat enterprise line item that can dominate small bills.

    Jahidul & his team — read this as a story first

    Because AUBRIA.ai holds real user data, Jahidul treats trust as part of the product, not an afterthought. The team switches on only the protection AUBRIA.ai actually needs — and notes which regimes they must answer to.

    1. At the edge they raise a WAF to filter malicious traffic before it reaches AUBRIA.ai, and — for the enterprise tier — Managed DDoS. Jahidul enables DDoS deliberately, since it's a flat line that can dominate a small bill.
    2. For everyday operations they turn on the tooling AUBRIA.ai needs: SIEM / audit logging for a tamper-evident trail, and a Secrets manager to vault API keys and credentials.
    3. They add KMS + encryption at rest so every stored byte is encrypted under managed keys — table stakes once you hold user data.
    4. To catch problems early they run Vulnerability scanning across hosts and schedule periodic Penetration testing to probe AUBRIA.ai the way an attacker would.
    5. Finally the obligations: the team ticks every Compliance framework that applies — SOC 2, GDPR, HIPAA and the rest. Each carries amortised audit and control cost, and selecting any one also switches on a continuous-compliance monitoring platform.

    Security here is mostly opt-in — Jahidul enables only what AUBRIA.ai's data and customers require, and Cumulus prices each control and every compliance obligation, so trust shows up honestly in the budget.

    Jahidul & his team — read this as a story first

    This is where most of AUBRIA.ai's cost — and most of its uncertainty — actually lives. As an AI-native product, its bill is driven less by servers than by tokens, embeddings, GPU-hours and the agentic loops behind every answer. So Jahidul slows the team down and they model the AI stack capability by capability: each child card below is priced on its own and rolls up into the single AI / ML line. First they choose their altitude — a quick ballpark, or the detailed breakdown — never both, so nothing is double-counted.

    How one request flows through AUBRIA.ai's AI stack
    Follow one user request
    1. A user asks AUBRIA.ai a question — maybe with an image or PDF attached. Any non-text input is metered by D · Multimodal (image, PDF, speech-to-text) before the model ever sees it.
    2. AUBRIA.ai first looks up what it knows. C · Retrieval & RAG embeds the query, searches the vector database, and reranks the best chunks — the knowledge the answer will be grounded in.
    3. Those chunks, plus the system prompt, the conversation history and the user query, are stitched into one prompt — the input tokens counted by B · Inference. A prompt-cache hit bills the repeated parts cheaper.
    4. The prompt goes to a model — but not always the same one. B routes traffic across Model A / B / C by share, and for agentic tasks it loops several LLM calls and tool calls per request, each adding tokens.
    5. Those tokens are served one of two ways: per-token through a provider API, or on AUBRIA.ai's own GPUs via E · Self-hosted serving — where GPU count × hours and the KV-cache decide how many users one card can hold at once.
    6. The answer streams back; if AUBRIA.ai speaks, D meters text-to-speech on the way out.
    7. Off to the side, two loops run continuously: F · Training periodically fine-tunes the models this pipeline uses (offline GPU-hours), and G · Operations evaluates quality, moderates content and keeps the flow observable.
    8. Every one of those meters rolls up into the single AI / ML cost line — or pick A · Simple estimate for a quick ballpark, which switches the detailed cards off so nothing is double-counted.

    10 Environments ?

    Non-production environments are the most commonly forgotten cost. Each carries a fraction of production sizing.

    Jahidul & his team — read this as a story first

    Almost there. Jahidul reminds the team that AUBRIA.ai isn't only the live site — the copies they build and test on cost real money too, and they're the line teams most often forget.

    1. Production is always on at 100% — the environment everything so far has sized. That one's a given.
    2. AUBRIA.ai also runs a Staging environment, a near-production rehearsal, which they budget at roughly 50% of prod.
    3. The team tests on QA / Test too — lighter again, about 25% of prod.
    4. And every engineer needs a Dev environment, the smallest at around 15% of prod.
    5. Toggling each one on adds that fraction of AUBRIA.ai's compute, databases, cache and monitoring — so the estimate covers the whole fleet, not just the live site.

    Non-prod is the cost teams most often miss. Jahidul switches on only the environments AUBRIA.ai actually keeps, and Cumulus adds each as its slice of production — turning an invisible line into a visible one.

    11 Commercial Model ?

    How you buy and what you actually pay. Commitments and spot discount steady compute; an enterprise/private discount, support tier, credits and tax then adjust the final invoice. Usage lines (egress, object storage, AI tokens) bill at rate regardless.

    Jahidul & his team — read this as a story first

    The last step. Everything so far sized what AUBRIA.ai needs; this card decides what the team actually pays. Jahidul brings in finance, because the very same architecture can carry wildly different invoices.

    1. First Jahidul picks where AUBRIA.ai runs — the Primary region — since the same stack can cost ~28% more in São Paulo than in US-East, and decides whether to add Active regions for latency or data residency.
    2. Next, how they buy steady compute — the Purchasing plan. On-demand is flexible but priciest; committing to a 1- or 3-year Savings Plan or Reserved term trades flexibility for up to ~50% off.
    3. They choose an Upfront payment — none, partial, or all — since paying more up front shaves a little extra off the rate.
    4. Not all compute should be locked in: they set Commitment coverage (the steady share they reserve) and Spot coverage (cheap, interruptible capacity for the fault-tolerant remainder).
    5. They set a Target CPU utilisation — the headroom AUBRIA.ai runs at; packing higher uses fewer cores but leaves less slack for spikes.
    6. If AUBRIA.ai has negotiated an Enterprise / private discount (an EDP), they apply that percentage across the bill.
    7. They pick a Support plan — none through Enterprise — a percentage of spend with a monthly minimum.
    8. Any Monthly credits (startup or cloud credits) are subtracted, and Sales tax / VAT added, to reach the real invoice.
    9. Finally a Monthly budget line lets Jahidul flag the moment AUBRIA.ai's plan drifts past what finance approved.
    10. The cloud isn't the whole invoice, though. The team adds third-party SaaS & licensing — the auth provider, email/SMS, payments, monitoring vendors and any commercial OS/DB licenses — billed by other companies, so it's taxed but never touched by the cloud discount.
    11. And the start was not free: they enter the one-time migration and setup spend once and amortise it over a year or three, so the monthly run-rate is honest without pretending a migration recurs forever.

    Usage lines — egress, object storage, AI tokens — bill at rate regardless. With this card Jahidul turns AUBRIA.ai's sized architecture into the number finance will actually see, and Cumulus shows it as a headline total, per-user economics and a multi-year projection he can take to the board.
